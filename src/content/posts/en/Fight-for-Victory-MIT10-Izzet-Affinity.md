## Foreword
I'm [許碩恩](https://x.com/KhoSekun), and I'm very grateful to [Guildmage’s Forum](https://guildmagesforum.tw/) for inviting me to write, giving me the chance to share my experience at this MIT. Besides winning MIT Round 10, I'm probably the current record holder for MIT top 8 heartbreak. Starting from the first round, I've played in MIT nine times, losing in the top 8 four of them, and only on the fifth top 8 did I finally earn a Pro Tour invitation. Besides briefly sharing my preparation and the course of the event for MIT Round 10, this article will also talk about what went through my mind during those four top 8 losses and share how I adjusted my mindset for competitive events, in the hope of helping you fellow Spikes.

## Preparation
For the past several rounds of the main event, I got in through MITQs run by stores. I remember the Round 10 MITQ happened to fall around the Chiba GP, and with changes in my personal life I had no energy to play any of them. In August, once I confirmed I was free the weekend of the main event, I decided to head north on Saturday and experience playing the LCQ.

After locking in the schedule I occasionally kept an eye on Modern metagame changes, and only started researching after [Spider-Man](https://scryfall.com/sets/spm) was released. I'm used to collecting information from Twitter (I strongly recommend adding [@fireshoes](https://x.com/fireshoes) to your list), and three weeks before MIT there happened to be a [PT EOE](https://melee.gg/Tournament/View/355905) whose metagame I could reference. Plenty of sideboard guides appear after an event like that, which is a big plus for a solo player like me who doesn't have time to actually practice.

With limited time and resources, settling on a deck early is what gives you enough time to practice. Ideally I start practicing two weeks before the event and stop changing decks once I've decided, at the latest one week before. Getting in five to ten MO Leagues, that is 25 to 50 best-of-three matches of practice, already makes me quite satisfied.

Considering cost-effectiveness, I usually pick a deck by three principles: the archetype suits how I play, it sits in a good spot in the metagame, and I'm not missing many physical cards. The decks I initially considered were Esper blink, Goryo, and Affinity. I already owned most of the physical cards for all three and had piloted them, and they all did well at PT EOE.

As for deck type, I know very well that I'm not good at resource-trading games. In midrange or control matchups that drag on I inevitably make mistakes, I often can't stabilize in an unfavorable position, and it's hard to beat players who have practiced more. So I choose aggro or combo decks that can proactively create problems for the opponent to solve, pressuring them into mistakes, and ideally end the game quickly to earn extra rest time.

So I ruled out Goryo and Blink directly before actually trying them. For the former, after losing {% mtglink "Grief" %} and gaining {% mtglink "Quantum Riddler" %}, it looked like it had shifted from a combo deck toward a midrange deck. The latter is a midrange deck that gains resources, and on top of that my experience of making the top 8 of MIT Round 8 with White-Black and immediately losing to the same archetype was unpleasant, so naturally I ended up choosing Affinity as my deck.

## Deck Adjustments
I thought back to ten years ago, when the first Modern deck I built for the WMCQ was Affinity. The deck now only keeps low-cost artifacts like {% mtglink "Memnite" %} , {% mtglink "Ornithopter" %} , {% mtglink "Springleaf Drum" %} , {% mtglink "Thoughtcast" %} , and the {% mtglink "Mox Opal" %} that cost 900 back then, but the core spirit is unchanged: a high-risk, high-reward glass cannon deck, except the payoffs have changed from {% mtglink "Arcbound Ravager" %} and {% mtglink "Cranial Plating" %} to the more stable and harder-to-interact-with {% mtglink "Pinnacle Emissary" %} and {% mtglink "Kappa Cannoneer" %} .

The current metagame also has a similarity with ten years ago: every deck brings sideboard cards against Affinity, which indirectly proves that Affinity is currently a glass cannon deck of high enough power. {% mtglink "Kappa Cannoneer" %} is a Commander card powerful enough to kill in Legacy, and EOE added {% mtglink "Pinnacle Emissary" %} to give the deck nut draws, with the capital to race combo decks, while {% mtglink "Urza’s Saga" %} / {% mtglink "Thoughtcast" %} let it fight resource battles against midrange or control decks. In addition, the maindeck can carry zero-cost artifacts that disrupt graveyards and sweep the board, and the land {% mtglink "Sink into Stupor" %} can serve as a universal answer. You could say this environment is Affinity's glory moment.

Unfortunately, before MIT the format hadn't evolved to the stage where you could copy a list mindlessly. A little analysis of the PT EOE decklists shows that even professional players hadn't yet found the best build. The one that drew the most attention was Shuhei Nakamura, who went undefeated on Day 1. The highlight of the Japanese team's Affinity was the combination of {% mtglink "Weapons Manufacturing" %} plus {% mtglink "Claws of Gix" %}, which performed well on the feature match table, but unfortunately Nakamura lost two win-ins and didn't make the top 8. This was also the deck most people copied after the event, and in the two weeks that followed, the same 75-card list kept making top 8 in MO Challenges.

![Nakamura Shuhei](https://images.guildmagesforum.tw/posts/Fight-for-Victory-MIT10-Izzet-Affinity/0cfc297e69ea.webp)

But looking more carefully at the PT EOE constructed decklists, another build of Affinity also did very well. Alex Friedrichsen and David Inglis's Team Handshake played a full set of {% mtglink "Springleaf Drum" %}, {% mtglink "Memnite" %}, {% mtglink "Ornithopter" %} and moved {% mtglink "Metallic Rebuke" %} to the sideboard, maximizing the chance of a nut draw.

![Team Handshake](https://images.guildmagesforum.tw/posts/Fight-for-Victory-MIT10-Izzet-Affinity/f02c339532c8.webp)

Just by looking, it's really impossible to confirm which build is better, but a few differences in construction details can be found.
Number of zero-cost artifacts: counting {% mtglink "Engineered Explosives" %} and {% mtglink "Mox Opal" %}, the Japanese list ran only 18, while Handshake ran 22.
Number of mana sources: the Japanese list has 17 lands (land-fetching lands) + 6 artifacts, while Handshake has 16 lands (card-draw lands) + 8 artifacts.
Number of payoffs: the Japanese list has two more {% mtglink "Weapons Manufacturing" %}.
Number of card-draw spells: the Japanese list has two more {% mtglink "Thought Monitor" %}.

Simply put, the Japanese build is greedier, using relatively few cheap artifacts to support more payoffs, and with too few artifacts drawn, it has a higher chance of getting stuck with uncastable spells. Handshake's build is quite conservative: although it has fewer payoffs, it doesn't get stuck, and the deck has higher consistency.

I started adjusting from Shuhei's deck. At first I changed the land-fetching land setup into card-draw lands plus fast lands, then tried not playing {% mtglink "Springleaf Drum" %} and {% mtglink "Memnite" %} at all, bringing {% mtglink "Mox Amber" %} plus a maindeck {% mtglink "Emry, Lurker of the Loch" %} instead, and then tried some odd tech I saw on MO, including {% mtglink "Vexing Bauble" %}, {% mtglink "Expressive Iteration" %}, {% mtglink "Salvage Titan" %} and so on. After three or four MO Leagues of experimenting, my conclusion was: {% mtglink "Weapons Manufacturing" %} can raise the deck's burst ceiling and helps a lot in midrange matchups, but the Japanese build is really too greedy, too few zero-cost artifacts makes the payoffs easily stuck, and on top of that in post-board games I often have to swap out the useless {% mtglink "Tormod’s Crypt" %} or {% mtglink "Engineered Explosives" %}, which makes the deck run clunkier.

Later I found the primer written by Team Handshake ([link](https://moxfield.com/decks/W88OoM08rkuD2BLs5HODDQ/primer)), which made the sideboarding logic clearer. The build I finally entered started from the Handshake construction, cut down the low-impact {% mtglink "Springleaf Drum" %} / {% mtglink "Memnite" %} / {% mtglink "Ornithopter" %}, added the {% mtglink "Weapons Manufacturing" %} package, and brought {% mtglink "Ashiok, Dream Render" %} in the sideboard to give Titan a little respect. By the time the deck was settled it was already Friday night, and I could only squeeze in one more League. In total I played nine MO League matches before the event, with a so-so win rate of 55.5%, so I really didn't have much confidence.

## Takeaways and Review
Actually, before this event I didn't have high expectations as in the past, and I never expected to win the title. Perhaps getting [Aerith Gainsborough](https://scryfall.com/card/ppro/2025-3/aerith-gainsborough) already felt like a win, or maybe I've failed enough times, but I stayed in a calm state of mind all day. All I can say is that my luck that day was really smooth, the deck helped a lot, there were a few key topdecks, and I never mulliganed down to five. The pairings were relatively favorable and none of the opponents had their ceiling draws, and the occasional mistakes didn't affect the result of any game except the first one. At the moment of winning the title I was quite happy, but also calmer than I expected, and actually the moment I won the quarterfinal and broke the curse, I felt more excited.

There is a neuropsychological theory that the pain failure brings to the human brain is far stronger than the satisfaction success brings. My experience in MIT made me feel this argument firsthand: compared with the sense of achievement from winning the title, the frustration from losing in the top 8 four times in the past was disproportionately high. I still remember every failure. The first time, I made the top 8 with Phoenix and was on the play but got beaten by a smoothly drawing Esper control, and afterward I heard that a top 4 finish would have earned a PT invitation, which made it even more sickening. The second time, with Infect against Grixis midrange, it was a relatively favorable matchup but I ran into a strong player, and on top of that I stumbled on lands in the key game. The third time, again with Infect, I ran into 剋星思源 piloting Boros convoke, and I felt I would lose before we even started. The fourth time, with White-Black midrange against the same archetype, I started to clown around and self-destruct as soon as my first draws went poorly. Each time I left Taipei on the high-speed rail my mood was very negative, I blamed everything, and I wondered what the point was of spending my time playing cards. Especially the feeling of having had many chances but not seizing them, with unnecessary expectations dashed creating excessive disappointment; these poorly handled emotions clearly affected my performance on the spot.

All TCGs, including MTG, have a heavier random component than other competitions such as sports, esports, and chess. A player who can maintain a 60 to 70 percent win rate over the long term is considered a top expert, but even so that doesn't necessarily translate into tournament placements, and over the long run everyone's experience is losing more than winning. Rationally we know the part we can control is limited, but the brain's original design makes us care too much about winning and losing. The feeling of losing is so painful that humans involuntarily avoid it, so the brain doesn't have to bear this unpleasantness: for example, deliberately covering up a mistake out of embarrassment when we notice it, fearing onlookers will see; being overly conservative from fear of losing an advantage; or even giving up on winning early to get rid of the discomfort of a disadvantage sooner. Quite a few people can recognize the effect their mindset has on a match, but overcoming it is very hard and takes enough awareness, experience, and practice.

Since winning and losing can't be controlled and failure is an unavoidable common experience, then besides not giving up and continuing to try, how do we maximize our chances of winning? I think we need to let go of the obsession with winning and losing and not focus our attention on parts we can't control, such as complaining about bad pairings, losing the die roll, how smoothly the opponent draws, or how unsmoothly we draw. Instead, focus attention on the parts we can control, including deck choice, practice, and health management beforehand, and then make every decision in the match well, such as mulligans, triggers, attacks and defenses, big-picture judgment, and communicating with the opponent. Accept that you can't be perfect, but do your best to enjoy the process, improve a little each time, enjoy the match as much as possible, and leave the final outcome to fate.

At least at MIT Round 10, the mindset shift above did bring me a winning result. Besides good luck, I managed my health a bit better, my stamina improved, and I could focus on making every decision well. My emotional swings that day were also small: when things went well I didn't get overexcited, and after making a mistake I didn't stay stuck in frustration. Even when I drew the first game because of an error, I could accept the result and steadily continue to the next game. In the all-important quarterfinal, although I was a bit nervous about extending my streak of crashing out, I stayed calm and focused on the present, and I kept that up until the end of the finals.

I believe everyone, like me, has struggled and given up in the process of repeated frustration, may be full of self-doubt, but still enjoys this game and will keep challenging it in the future. Thanks to 佳儒 and 蘋果 who helped find cards, the Tainan friends who shared the victory that night, all the respectable opponents and judges, and all the store owners who keep running events, especially 紙牌屋 Toley and 遊戲平方's 郭老闆. I feel we are very lucky to live in this era: we can play small events at the card store for promo cards, find strangers to practice with on MO, play MTGA for prize money or boxes, and each year there are three high-level competitive in-person events to play. I hope everyone can keep their own pace and challenge themselves, and someday we can go abroad together to play Pro Tour events.
