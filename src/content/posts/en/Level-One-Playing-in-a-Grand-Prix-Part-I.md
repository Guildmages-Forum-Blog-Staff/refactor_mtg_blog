## Article Translation: "Level One": Playing in a Grand Prix: Part I

[Link to the original](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/feature/playing-grand-prix-part-i-2015-05-18)

Wizards of the Coast column: Feature
Date: May 18, 2015
Author: Reid Duke

Twelve years<sup>[Note 1](#quote1)</sup> later, I still remember my first Magic: The Gathering Grand Prix (GP)<sup>[Note 2](#quote2)</sup> as if it happened yesterday. In fact, I'm pretty sure the memory will stay with me until the day I die. While other kids my age spent all year waiting to vacation at Disneyland or to watch their favorite sports team play, I chose to enter my first big Magic tournament. The greatest happiness I could imagine was simply getting to enter another one.

I hope everyone who aspires to play in a Grand Prix gets the chance to try it at least once. With the Grand Prix schedule expanding, it looks like these opportunities will become more frequent in the near future<sup>[Note 3](#quote3)</sup>. The Las Vegas Grand Prix, coming up in a few weeks, will be the biggest Magic tournament of all time. So far, there's truly nothing that compares to the excitement of gathering with such an enormous crowd to share a common passion.

Playing in a Grand Prix may well turn out to be a truly outstanding experience in your life. However, big tournaments can be hectic, stressful, and emotional. In this two-part series, I want to offer as much advice as I can to help you bring out your best in the tournament and avoid anything that might keep the experience from being perfect.

![](https://images.guildmagesforum.tw/posts/Level-One-Playing-in-a-Grand-Prix-Part-I/e45786f56dd6.webp)

### Setting Goals

Most people enter big tournaments for a combination of three reasons. The first is to do well in the tournament itself. Maybe they have their eye on the prize money, or maybe they simply enjoy challenging themselves and showing their best. The second is to learn and grow as a player (perhaps to do well in future tournaments). The third is simply to have fun. None of these motivations is better or worse than the others. Personally, I play in tournaments for about an equal mix of all three.

The reality is that these three are closely related. The first prerequisite for achieving all of these goals is to have a healthy, positive attitude. In my experience, you're more likely to play well when you're enjoying yourself. Excessive frustration usually ruins your chances of getting any value out of the experience.

So the first thing I want to cover is setting goals for yourself as you go into the tournament. Different people have different ways of bringing out their best, so what works for me may be quite different from what works for you. Even so, clarifying your intentions and your expectations for the tournament will help steer you toward the experience you're hoping for.

My best advice on this topic is that your goals shouldn't be rooted entirely in your final placement in the tournament. Remember, Magic is a challenging game with a lot of randomness; no one in the world performs well in every tournament they enter.

In addition, setting a bottom-line goal for yourself carries two particular risks. Suppose you set the goal: "My goal is to make Day Two!" First, if you fail to reach it, you might feel crushed by pressure and disappointment, which could mean missing out on the other good things the tournament experience can offer. Second, you make it harder for yourself to exceed that goal! Imagine you find yourself at 7 wins and 1 loss. You've already reached your goal of making Day Two. But you've been playing exceptionally well, and you might even be close to a shot at an excellent finish! If so, this is the time to redouble your efforts, not to feel satisfied with what you've already accomplished.

The most helpful goals are the ones you can work toward no matter how you're doing in the tournament. For example: "My goal is to not get distracted while I play." Or: "My goal is to make careful mulligan decisions." Or: "My goal is to miss as few triggered abilities as possible."

![](https://images.guildmagesforum.tw/posts/Level-One-Playing-in-a-Grand-Prix-Part-I/24ecc9891a08.webp)

That said, some people (myself included) are very goal-oriented and need goals to help them play their best. If you're like me, I recommend using benchmarks. "My goal is to win every match I play, but if I can finish 6-3 or better, I'll also be proud of myself." Or: "If I don't win at least half my matches, I'll consider changing decks next time."

Here are the goals I personally set for the Las Vegas Grand Prix:

- If I can win at least nine matches (not counting byes), I'll be proud of myself.
- I want to make the fullest possible use of my sideboard.
- I want to stay focused and not get discouraged if I hit a losing streak.
- I want to play out every round, even if I no longer have a chance at any prizes. (More about this in [Part II](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/feature/playing-grand-prix-part-ii-2015-05-26).)

Of course, every player will have a different set of goals. What are your goals for your next tournament?

### Being at Your Best

I've seen some players practice incredibly hard for a tournament. They care so much, and they want so badly to do well. Yet as the tournament approaches, some of them ruin their efforts by not taking care of themselves. Maybe they stayed out too late the night before and didn't get enough sleep. Maybe they were late to a round and got a game loss. In whatever way, they wasted their chance to perform well for the wrong reasons.

No matter how much benefit practice brings, it won't help you unless you're ready to show up at your best on the day of the tournament.

Do everything you can to make sure you're in good physical condition on tournament day. Everyone needs sleep, food, and water. Go to bed early, get a good night's sleep, and leave time for breakfast before the tournament starts. Everyone may also have individual needs. Do you like coffee in the morning? Do you have medication you need to carry with you or take before leaving? Is there a particular song or ritual that helps you get in the zone? Personally, I like to stretch a bit in the morning, especially if I just got off a plane or went through a long car ride the day before. Otherwise, sitting for so many consecutive hours during the tournament makes me uncomfortable, which hurts my performance.

In a perfect world, you'd start paying attention to putting yourself in peak condition several days in advance. I have a health-conscious friend who claims the amount he sleeps on Wednesday and Thursday nights has more to do with how he does in Saturday's tournament than how much he slept the night before. Don't forget, this is a long haul. If tweaking your diet slightly or doing a moderate amount of cardio can improve your stamina, those details can become the key to winning or losing.

Equally important is maintaining a positive, focused mental state. As I said before, disappointment and frustration will ruin the rest of your tournament, and so will the effects of stress.

Anything you can do on Friday night to make Saturday morning less of a scramble is a good idea. Double-check that you've successfully registered. If it's a constructed event, build your deck and pack your bag beforehand. If you're traveling with friends, make sure everyone's schedules line up. You already have enough to deal with during the tournament; you don't need extra stress, such as hurriedly searching for a card you need or waiting around for a friend who thought the tournament started an hour later.

![](https://images.guildmagesforum.tw/posts/Level-One-Playing-in-a-Grand-Prix-Part-I/aba29707ed66.webp)

By the same token, don't pick travel companions who will keep you from playing at your best. If something a friend does bothers you, tell them directly; if they carry on regardless, consider choosing someone else next time. You don't need to cut them out of your life over it, but if you find that every trip to a tournament leaves you mentally and physically drained because of it, you absolutely must make a change.

Whether it's troubles with family, work, or school, set them aside when you head out to play, and deal with them when the tournament is over and you're home. Since you've decided to clear a weekend to play Magic: The Gathering, enjoy it wholeheartedly! You'll definitely enjoy it more and play better if you don't have to worry, while playing, about whether your boyfriend or girlfriend at home is mad at you.

Finally, your pre-tournament practice has to stop at a reasonable point. Playing a few games in your hotel room the night before the tournament for fun is fine, but practicing until the middle of the night is usually a bad idea. It only makes you weary and anxious, and what you need instead is to keep your mind in its best shape. If you're practicing for a constructed event, you'll often fall into self-doubt at this point and start changing cards in your deck at random, or even changing your whole strategy at the last minute. In my experience, this often backfires. Personally, I like to practice heavily from Monday to Thursday, and on Friday I let myself relax.

### Dealing with Your Opponents

At a Magic: The Gathering tournament, especially a Grand Prix, you'll meet all kinds of opponents. They may be of any age, gender, or experience level, they may come from anywhere in the world, and sometimes they may even speak a different language from yours. The one thing you can be sure of is that they love Magic: The Gathering, just like you.

Don't let the intense competitive atmosphere make it hard to breathe. Of course, you're trying to beat each other, but the very fact that you're sitting across from someone playing Magic: The Gathering is a good reason to be friends, at least in my view. There's absolutely no reason to cower before your opponents or to get into serious conflicts with them. If they're nice to you (and the vast majority of opponents will be), you can be nice to them; if they're mean, unpleasant, or overly competitive, you can simply tune out anything that bothers you and focus on playing. It's easy, and I've been doing it for more than ten years!

Playing against strangers at a big tournament isn't actually much different from playing a friend at home. The most important thing is good communication and making sure both players are always perfectly clear on the current situation. Last week, I discussed in detail "the proper way to cast spells." Carry that meticulous precision through every action in the game, and insist that your opponent do the same.

Announce everything clearly. If you're not sure exactly what your opponent is doing, ask for clarification. If a turn gets complicated, take your time and go through it step by step.

I want to add one extra emphasis: you and your opponent should be very clear on both players' life totals. Use pen and paper to track both players' life totals. Whenever anyone's life total changes, confirm it out loud. If you see that your opponent has recorded a life total incorrectly, under the rules you are obligated to clear up the misunderstanding immediately. The last thing you want is to make a decision thinking you have 8 life when you actually have 6. Even worse, you don't want the life total discrepancy to persist for several turns, because by then it may be too late to correct.

![](https://images.guildmagesforum.tw/posts/Level-One-Playing-in-a-Grand-Prix-Part-I/9247cc89ff1b.webp)

### Playing By the Rules

A moment ago, I encouraged you to be friendly to your opponents. Also remember that you're in a competition, so the rules will be enforced strictly and precisely. Fortunately, these two things don't actually conflict as long as everyone is on the same page.

It goes without saying that take-backs are not allowed in a tournament. If you make a mistake and your opponent insists on enforcing it, he or she isn't being difficult with you; they're just following the rules. Never blame someone for following the rules in a tournament. If you don't want to be a cold-blooded player, you needn't force yourself to be; but once you step onto the tournament floor, you must be ready to approach your games with a serious and meticulous attitude.

This brings us to the troubling topic of cheating. Cheating does happen at Magic: The Gathering tournaments, and it would be naive to think otherwise. Fortunately, this doesn't need to leave you living in fear every time you play, and I'm confident in saying it won't ruin your tournament experience. Most players want to play a clean game, and the few who have bad intentions are held in check by judges and other players.

That said, it never hurts to protect yourself. Whenever Player A shuffles their own deck (whether before the match or after using cards like [Windswept Heath](https://scryfall.com/card/ktk/248/zht/%E9%A2%A8%E8%A5%B2%E8%8D%92%E5%9C%B0-(windswept-heath))), Player B is also permitted to shuffle Player A's deck. I encourage you to shuffle your opponent's deck. Likewise, if something looks suspicious, don't blindly trust that the other person simply meant no harm; be sure to clarify what's going on.

In these situations, it's important to remember that you are not accusing your opponent of cheating (accusing someone of cheating is very serious); you're just taking the normal precautions a tournament player should.

Next week, we'll discuss the role of judges, who can help you deal with the issues above and countless other tricky situations. Until then, I hope everyone puts the advice in this article to use so that you can have the best possible tournament experience.
