## Rating Scale

5.0: Must-take, unconditionally; gives you a near-winning advantage
4.5: Must-take if you're in the right colors; gives you a huge advantage
4.0: Worth a first pick, with excellent synergy with the color pair's theme; can be the deciding card for splashing, switching colors, or moving into a color pair
3.5: The best uncommons and commons in the set and other cards of sufficient strength, with good synergy with the color pair's theme; can be the key card for moving into a color pair
3.0: Important cards for a color pair's theme and other above-average cards; you'll want them in your final deck
2.5: Average, usually a decent curve filler
2.0-1.0: Below average; you generally prefer not to play them, but they can be filler when necessary (not too many)
0.5-0.0: Unplayable; basically never play them, as they will only help you lose

Comment:

This card is easy to misjudge. Seeing it is a one-mana removal spell, people apply it to the usage scenarios of other one-mana removal spells and then feel that giving the opponent a land is an unacceptable side effect.

That reasoning is fine in Constructed, but in Limited, where removal is limited, you need to pay more attention to the removal's range to decide where it belongs. For low-range removal like [Shock](https://scryfall.com/card/j25/598/shock), most of the time you'd indeed be spending it early on a two- or three-mana creature, and killing some unimportant small thing early in a game while giving the opponent a land is naturally an unacceptable side effect.

But with unconditional kill spells like [Murder](https://scryfall.com/card/j25/467/murder) or even [Swords to Plowshares](https://scryfall.com/card/blc/109/swords-to-plowshares), even if you have the mana you don't throw it at just any target; you use it on a truly key problem, and by the time that problem shows up the opponent's lands have usually grown to a certain number, so giving them one more land then really isn't that costly.

Taking all of the above together, even though this is a one-mana removal spell, it's not a filler to use casually early on; it's what lets you, once the game reaches the midgame, keep casting your powerful curve creatures while removing the opponent's key threats without losing much tempo.

## Conclusion

The mechanic themes of the white color pairs this time are black-white's Repartee and red-white's Flashback, though Flashback's emphasis is really on the red side, while white focuses more on Repartee.

Honestly, Repartee is rather hard to score, because in practice it leans toward a high-synergy aggro style of play. Each card on its own may not look impressive in power or versatility, but when the package is complete enough it can produce strong burst in the early turns, and some combat tricks you'd normally overlook get completely different scores under this archetype.

Although Repartee is a lot more flexible than mechanics like Heroic and Valiant that can only pump yourself (removal thrown at an opponent's creature triggers it too), the need for frequent spell support doesn't change, so if you plan to pick cards around Repartee as a theme, pay special attention to whether you can keep the trigger frequency up. Even if you aren't specifically drafting around Repartee, as long as you've invested in Repartee cards, it's still worth paying attention to how strong your trigger ability is. For many Prepare creatures, as long as the body itself is decent, even if the Prepare spell's effect isn't very important, being able to support Repartee is enough to be an extra bonus.
